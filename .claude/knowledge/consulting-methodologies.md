# Knowledge Domain: Consulting Methodologies

## 1. Core Concepts

- **Hypothesis-driven consulting.** Competent consulting work starts from an early, testable hypothesis about the answer, then gathers data to confirm, refine, or reject it — rather than open-ended, blank-slate data gathering that delays insight and wastes client time.
- **MECE structuring.** Breaking a problem into parts that are Mutually Exclusive and Collectively Exhaustive is the foundational discipline for both analysis (issue trees) and communication (structured findings) — sloppy structure produces sloppy thinking, not just sloppy slides.
- **The engagement lifecycle has distinct phases with distinct deliverables.** Scoping, diagnosis, design, piloting, implementation, and transition/handoff are different kinds of work requiring different client engagement and different success criteria — collapsing them into one undifferentiated "project" produces mismatched expectations.
- **Change management is a parallel workstream, not an afterthought.** Technical or analytical recommendations fail on adoption far more often than on analytical quality; a change-management plan (stakeholder map, communication plan, capability-building) must run alongside the technical work from day one.
- **The trusted advisor relationship is the actual product in many engagements.** Especially for a boutique firm, the client relationship — credibility, reliability, and intimacy earned over time — often determines repeat engagement and referenceability more than any single deliverable.

## 2. Terminology

- **MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive)** — a structuring principle ensuring analysis categories don't overlap and don't leave gaps.
- **Issue tree** — a MECE breakdown of a problem into its component sub-questions, used to structure analysis and workplanning.
- **Hypothesis-driven approach** — starting from an early best-guess answer and structuring data-gathering to test it, rather than gathering all available data before forming a view.
- **Pyramid Principle** — Barbara Minto's method for structuring communication: lead with the answer/conclusion, then support it with MECE grouped arguments, each backed by evidence.
- **RACI** — Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed; a matrix clarifying roles across an engagement or workstream.
- **Statement of Work (SOW)** — the document defining engagement scope, deliverables, timeline, and commercial terms.
- **Deliverable-based vs. time-and-materials engagement** — pricing/scoping models where the client pays for defined outputs versus pays for time spent, each with different risk allocation between consultant and client.
- **Stakeholder map** — a structured analysis of who is affected by or influences an engagement's outcome, their interests, and their level of influence, used to plan engagement and change-management activity.
- **Trusted advisor model** — a relationship-quality framework (Maister, Green, Galford): Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) ÷ Self-orientation — the more a consultant appears self-interested, the less trusted they become, regardless of competence.

## 3. Best Practices

- Start every engagement with an explicit hypothesis about the likely answer, and structure the workplan to test it efficiently rather than gathering data unboundedly.
- Structure findings using MECE issue trees before writing any client-facing narrative.
- Treat diagnosis, recommendation, and implementation planning as distinct phases with distinct deliverables and distinct client sign-offs, rather than one undifferentiated blob of "the project."
- Build a stakeholder map and change-management plan alongside any technical or analytical workstream from the outset, not as a follow-on activity after recommendations are finalized.
- Define success criteria and a Statement of Work before starting work, including what "done" looks like, to prevent scope creep and misaligned expectations.
- Lead every deliverable with the answer (Pyramid Principle), then support it — never bury the conclusion under a chronological narrative of the analysis process.

## 4. Frameworks

- **The Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto)** — structure communication top-down: state the answer first, then the MECE-grouped supporting arguments, each backed by evidence — the opposite of building up to a conclusion.
- **The classic consulting engagement lifecycle** — Scope → Diagnose → Design → Pilot → Implement → Transition. Each phase has a different client relationship dynamic and a different definition of a successful deliverable.
- **Kotter's 8-Step Change Model** — create urgency, build a guiding coalition, form a vision, communicate the vision, empower broad-based action, generate short-term wins, consolidate gains, anchor changes in culture — a standard reference model for the change-management workstream that should run parallel to any technical recommendation.
- **RACI matrix** — used for engagement governance, clarifying who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed at each stage, reducing ambiguity between consultant and client teams.
- **The Trusted Advisor model (Maister, Green, Galford)** — Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) ÷ Self-orientation; a practical diagnostic for why a client relationship is or isn't deepening over time.

## 5. Decision Trees

**Is this engagement diagnosis-only, or diagnosis-plus-implementation?**
- Does the client have internal capability and capacity to implement recommendations without ongoing support? → Diagnosis-only engagement, with a clearly scoped, deliverable-based SOW.
- Does the client lack internal capacity, or is the change complex/high-stakes enough to require hands-on implementation support? → Diagnosis-plus-implementation, with a phased SOW and distinct success criteria per phase.

**Time-and-materials or fixed-fee/deliverable-based pricing?**
- Is the scope well-understood and stable, with low risk of significant discovery-driven scope change? → Fixed-fee/deliverable-based pricing is viable and often preferred by the client for budget certainty.
- Is the scope genuinely uncertain at the outset (e.g., an open-ended diagnostic where findings will shape next steps)? → Time-and-materials, ideally with a not-to-exceed cap and a defined checkpoint to re-scope once the diagnostic phase concludes.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

- **"Boil the ocean" scoping** — attempting to address every possible angle of a problem with no prioritization, producing an unfocused engagement and a diluted deliverable.
- **Report-only delivery with no adoption plan** — producing a polished analytical report with no accompanying change-management or implementation plan, resulting in a deliverable that documents insight but changes nothing.
- **Skipping stakeholder mapping** — proceeding straight to analysis and recommendations without identifying who will need to be brought along, then encountering late-stage political resistance that could have been anticipated.
- **Recommendations disconnected from implementation capacity** — proposing changes the client organization cannot realistically execute given its actual resources, culture, or absorption capacity.
- **Scope creep with no SOW discipline** — allowing engagement scope to expand informally without renegotiating time, fee, or deliverables, eroding both profitability and the clarity of what was actually promised.

## 7. KPIs

- Engagement client satisfaction / NPS score
- Recommendation adoption rate — % of recommendations actually implemented within a defined follow-up period (e.g., 12 months)
- Time-to-value — how quickly the client realizes tangible benefit from the engagement
- Referenceability rate — % of clients willing to serve as a reference or case study afterward
- Repeat engagement rate — a strong proxy for trusted-advisor relationship quality
- Scope-change frequency — how often engagement scope had to be renegotiated mid-project, an indicator of scoping discipline

## 8. Common Mistakes

- Over-indexing on analytical polish while under-indexing on implementation feasibility, producing an impressive but unimplementable recommendation.
- Failing to secure a client-side sponsor with genuine authority, leaving the engagement without an internal champion once consultants are no longer in the room.
- Treating change management as a communications afterthought rather than a parallel workstream planned from day one alongside the technical work.
- Not defining what "done" looks like before starting, leading to open-ended engagements and client dissatisfaction regardless of work quality.
- Ignoring the client organization's realistic absorption capacity — recommending more simultaneous change than the organization can execute, guaranteeing partial or failed implementation regardless of how sound the underlying analysis was.

## 9. References to Standards

- **Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle** — the industry-standard reference text for structured business communication, originating from McKinsey practice.
- **Institute of Management Consultants (IMC) Code of Ethics and Professional Standards** — a professional standards body for management consulting practice.
- **ISO 20700** — guidelines for management consultancy service delivery, the closest thing to a formal international standard specifically for consulting engagement practice.
- **PMI / PMBOK** — project management standards relevant to any implementation-phase workstream within a consulting engagement.
- **Prosci ADKAR model** — a widely used, measurable change-management framework (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement) for assessing individual and organizational change readiness.

## 10. Future Trends

- AI-assisted diagnosis (faster data synthesis, automated issue-tree generation, rapid draft analysis) shifting consultant value further toward judgment, client relationship, and implementation support rather than raw data-gathering and synthesis.
- Growing client demand for embedded or fractional consulting models (ongoing, part-time integration into the client team) over discrete, bounded project engagements.
- Movement toward outcome-based or value-based pricing models, replacing pure time-and-materials billing as clients seek tighter alignment between consultant incentives and actual results.
- Increasing integration of change-management and technology-implementation workstreams as AI tools themselves become the deliverable (e.g., a deployed agentic system, see `agentic-ai.md`) rather than only a written recommendation — requiring consulting methodology to absorb technical deployment and governance discipline (see `ai-governance.md`) as a standard part of the engagement lifecycle, not a separate specialty.
